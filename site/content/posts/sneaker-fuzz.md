> The way U.S. shoe tariffs work is that there is a book, [Chapter 64 of the Harmonized Tariff Schedule](https://hts.usitc.gov/reststop/file?release=currentRelease&filename=Chapter%2064), and the book sorts every shoe by what the top is made of, what the bottom is made of, how the top is attached to the bottom and what the shoe is worth at the port, and then assigns each combination a duty rate that seems to have been drawn from a hat. A canvas sneaker with a rubber sole and a strip of rubber wrapped around the edge (a "foxing band," if you bill by the hour) goes in heading 6404. If it is valued at $3 to $6.50 a pair it lands in [subheading 6404.11.79](https://hts.usitc.gov/search?query=6404.11) and owes 90 cents a pair plus 37.5%. From $6.50 to $12 it is 6404.11.89, 90 cents plus 20%. Under $3 it is 48%. Over $12 it is a flat 20%.

> A slipper has a cloth bottom, and shoes with textile outer soles go in [heading 6405](https://hts.usitc.gov/search?query=6405.20), "other footwear," where a cotton upper pays 7.5% (6405.20.30) and any other textile upper pays 12.5% (6405.20.90).

> How does the book know what the bottom is made of? Note 4(b) to the chapter says the sole is whatever material has "the greatest surface area in contact with the ground." So if you are a sneaker company you read that and think: What if we glued a thin layer of felt over a bit more than half of the rubber? Then at the moment the shoe meets a customs officer it is a slipper. A block later it is a sneaker again, but nobody collects tariffs on the sidewalk.

> This is why a new pair of Converse Chuck Taylors is [fuzzy underneath](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/this-is-why-your-converse-sneakers-have-felt-on-the-bottom-6016648/), and Converse is not alone. [One patent for a fabric-bottomed shoe](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6571491) says it out loud: Put fabric on more than half of the ground-contacting surface and the shoe is "classifiable under subheading 6405.20.90, which carries a 12.5% rate of duty." I love a patent that lists paying less tax among the advantages of the invention.

> The math is pleasant. Take a canvas high-top that clears customs at $10 a pair, which is my guess for a shoe like this. As a sneaker it owes 90 cents plus $2, so $2.90, which is 29% of the value of the shoe. As a slipper with a cotton upper it owes 75 cents. A few cents of felt saves $2.15 a pair. With a synthetic upper at 12.5% it saves $1.65. Ship a million pairs and you have somewhere around $2 million and a lot of lint.

> I should say that the internet's favorite version of this is "[37.5% down to 3%](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/this-is-why-your-converse-sneakers-have-felt-on-the-bottom-6016648/)," sometimes with a claim that it is worth [$15 million or $20 million a year](https://www.tarifflens.ai/blog/tariff-engineering-sneakers-apparel) to Converse. I can't find a 3% line for these shoes in the schedule, or a source for the millions; Converse does not publish its customs values. What the schedule says is 7.5% and 12.5%. The sneaker subheading also has no fuzz rate above $12 a pair, so this is a trick for cheap shoes, where "cheap" means the price at the port and not the price at the mall.

> For a long time Customs was fine with this. A 2002 headquarters ruling, [HQ 964978](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/964978), looked at a women's slip-on with fabric glued to the sole and found that the fabric was a real constituent of the sole and not a disguise, and that it did not need any commercial purpose. The general rule of tariff law is that you are taxed on the thing you import, in the condition you import it, and you are allowed to build the thing with the tax in mind.

> Eventually the government got tired of fuzzy basketball shoes. In 2010 Treasury asked the International Trade Commission to look into it ([Investigation No. 1205-8](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2010-04-13/html/2010-8360.htm)), and in late 2011 [Presidential Proclamation 8742](https://www.barnesrichardson.com/cbp-requests-advance-public-comments-on-proposed-administration-of-additional-us-note-5-to-htsus-chapter-64) added Additional U.S. Note 5 to Chapter 64: When you decide what the sole is made of, ignore any textile that lacks the "durability and strength" a sole normally needs. Then Customs had to decide how fuzzy is too fuzzy, and since November 2013 the answer is [a lab test](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/client-advisories/new-customs-requirements-for-classifying-footwear-with-textile-material-on-outer-soles). It runs an abrasion standard, ISO 20871, on three samples cut from the sole, and if any textile is still visible on any one of them, the sole is textile. Actual slippers skip the test.

> So that closed the loophole? Well. Changes made this way are supposed to be [roughly rate-neutral](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/19/3005), and the shoe industry [showed up to say so](https://www.aafaglobal.org/SFTP/Letters_Comments/Archived/062510footwearoutsole.pdf). The same round of amendments created new lines inside the sneaker heading for shoes whose soles are mostly textile that Note 5 tells you to ignore: 6404.11.71 and 6404.11.81 for cotton uppers, at 7.5%, and 6404.11.75 and 6404.11.85 for other textile uppers, at 12.5%. Those are the slipper rates. The government looked at the fake slippers, ruled that they were sneakers, and then created a category of sneaker called, more or less, "fake slipper," which is taxed like a slipper. The felt stays on the shoe.

> The carve-outs don't cover everything. Target imported toddler high-tops in 2013 with mostly plastic uppers and fabric-covered soles and asked for 12.5% under 6405.90.90. The Customs lab ran the test, the fabric was gone from all three specimens after 40 meters, and in 2018 ruling [H295154](https://rulings.cbp.gov/ruling/H295154) left the shoes in 6402.91.80 at 90 cents plus 20%.

> None of this touches the newer tariffs. The emergency-powers tariffs [died at the Supreme Court in February](https://www.bakerdonelson.com/trade-policy-shifts-ieepa-tariffs-end-section-122-begins-and-sections-301-and-232-activity-grows), the 10% Section 122 surcharge that replaced them expired in July, and since July 24 there are Section 301 "forced labor" [tariffs of 10% or 12.5%](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/07/and-the-tariff-beat-goes-on) depending on the country, on top of the older Section 301 duty on Chinese shoes. Those mostly depend on where the shoe is from. Nobody has yet found a fabric you can glue to a shoe to make it come from somewhere else.

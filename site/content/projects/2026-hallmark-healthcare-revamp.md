Labor is the single largest line item for almost every hospital and health system in the country, and it is also the one they understand the least. The people who actually deliver care arrive through a tangle of arrangements: employed staff, internal float pools, per-diem workers, agency and travel clinicians, and physicians working under individually negotiated compensation agreements. Each of those arrangements is tracked somewhere different, in scheduling systems, timekeeping systems, credentialing files, contract repositories, and payroll, and almost none of it reconciles. The result is that a health system can rarely answer questions as basic as what a filled shift really costs, or whether a physician was paid what their contract actually says.

[Hallmark Health Care Solutions](https://hallmarkhcs.com/) builds the workforce platform that a great many of those health systems run on, and I led the data and AI efforts for the redesign of its two flagship product lines, Flexible Workforce and Physician Enterprise. The first job was unglamorous and unavoidable: building a shared data foundation underneath both products, so that workers, positions, shifts, rates, contracts, and payments meant the same thing everywhere, with lineage back to the source systems that produced them. Without that, every model built on top is a very confident guess.

With that in place, we could build things that were genuinely useful. On the Flexible Workforce side, that meant forecasting shift-level demand far enough ahead that units could fill from their own float pools instead of paying agency premiums at the last minute, along with rate benchmarking that showed what a given specialty and market actually clears at rather than what a vendor claims. On the Physician Enterprise side, it meant turning dense compensation agreements into machine-checkable terms, so productivity, quality incentives, and call coverage could be evaluated against what each contract literally requires, and discrepancies could be surfaced before they became audit findings or awkward conversations.

Throughout, the design constraint was that a person stays accountable for every consequential decision. These systems recommend, explain, and show their work, but a staffing manager still fills the shift and a compensation administrator still approves the payment. In a domain where the outputs move real money and shape who is at the bedside, that is not a limitation to engineer around; it is the point.
